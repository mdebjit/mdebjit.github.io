In 1992, two psychologists named Peter Ditto and David Lopez handed a group of students a strip of treated paper and asked them to rub it in their own saliva.

The strip tested for an enzyme called thioamine acetylase. People who lack it, the students were told, end up "relatively susceptible to a variety of pancreatic disorders" later in life. The colour would take somewhere between ten seconds and a minute to come through. Once it was clear, they were to put the strip in a bag and seal it up.

Half of them were told that a colour change meant they had the problem.

The other half were told that no colour change meant they had the problem.

The strip was a fake. It was an ordinary piece of paper and it never changed colour for anybody, which meant that every single student in that room sat watching exactly the same nothing happen. For half of them, that nothing was a clean bill of health. For the other half, it was the worst news they had heard all year.

The ones who got the bad news sat there longer.

They were also much more likely to test themselves a second time. Fifty-two percent of them did, against eighteen percent of the students who had just been told they were fine. Most of them did it by dipping the very same strip back into their own saliva, as though the enzyme might have reconsidered.

And they started finding reasons. They had slept badly. They had eaten something strange. They were stressed, and everyone knows what stress does to a person. On reflection, they decided, this test was probably not all that accurate anyway.

Somewhere in that room, a nineteen-year-old with a wet piece of paper invented peer review.

Not one of them said the result was a lie. Nobody shouted. Nobody stormed out.

They just checked it more carefully. That is the whole thing. They just checked it more carefully than the people who liked what they got.

Ditto and Lopez put it into a single sentence, and it is the kind of sentence you end up thinking about years later:

*"Several doctors may be required to provide convincing proof of illness, but one is generally enough to provide convincing proof of health."*

## You have seen this before. You saw it in a meeting.

Someone is presenting two sets of numbers.

The first set belongs to the pilot that this room has been defending for six months. It came back flat.

The second set belongs to an idea nobody in particular sponsored. It came back strong.

Now watch which one gets the questions.

The flat one gets all of them. Was the sample big enough? Did it run over the holidays? Was the tracking set up properly? Can we give it one more quarter before we call it?

Every single one of those is a good question. That is exactly the problem.

The strong one gets a nod. Then it gets a slide.

Nobody in that room is lying. Nobody has decided to ignore the evidence. They are doing something far more ordinary than that, and far harder to catch.

They are being rigorous. They are just not being rigorous evenly.

Thomas Gilovich once compressed all of this into one sentence, and like the Ditto and Lopez sentence, it has outlived most of the research around it:

> "For desired conclusions, in other words, it is as if we ask ourselves, 'Can I believe this?', but for unpalatable conclusions we ask, 'Must I believe this?'"

*Can I* is an easy question. One decent reason will settle it.

*Must I* is a hard question. Any flaw, any alternative explanation, any small complaint about the sample size, and you get to set the answer down and walk away from it for a while.

Two questions. Two entirely different standards of proof. One person, who will tell you with total sincerity that they applied the same standard to both.

From the inside they feel identical. Both feel like honesty. That is the trick of it, and it is why nobody ever catches themselves at it.

There is a thermostat on your scepticism. You have been adjusting it all your life and you have never once noticed your hand on the dial.

<div class="takeaway"><span>Takeaway</span><p>Bias almost never shows up as a bad question. It shows up as a very good question, asked in only one direction.</p></div>

## What is actually going on

The person to thank for understanding any of this is Ziva Kunda.

In 1990 she published a review in *Psychological Bulletin* that rescued an idea most of her field had quietly given up on. The critics of the 1970s had a good argument: every time somebody thought they had caught a person's wishes bending their thinking, you could tell the story a second way, colder and duller, and the second story fit just as well. Kunda agreed the dispute "could not be solved, at least in the attribution paradigm."

So she stopped arguing about whether it was real.

She sat down and worked out how it would have to work instead, which is a deeply sensible move that almost nobody makes.

Motivation does not switch your reasoning off, she said. It runs through your reasoning. Same memory. Same logic. Same standards of evidence. Just aimed.

She separated two kinds of goal, and was careful to credit the distinction to Arie Kruglanski rather than herself:

- **Accuracy goals.** You want to get it right. These make you think harder, and better.
- **Directional goals.** You want to get to one particular answer. These quietly decide which facts turn up in your head, and which rules you apply to them once they arrive.

Note that the first is not an antidote to the second. They are both motivations. Only one of them has somewhere it needs to be.

And then Kunda wrote the line that makes this idea useful instead of merely cynical:

{{< admonition type="quote" title="Kunda (1990), Psychological Bulletin" >}}
"People do not seem to be at liberty to conclude whatever they want to conclude merely because they want to. Rather, I propose that people motivated to arrive at a particular conclusion attempt to be rational and to construct a justification of their desired conclusion **that would persuade a dispassionate observer**."
{{< /admonition >}}

Read that twice. That is the whole difference between motivated reasoning and simply wanting things.

You cannot decide that the quarter was fine. You cannot decide that the lump is nothing, or that your best engineer is not about to hand in her notice. You have to build a case. A real one, with real evidence in it, good enough to survive a stranger who does not care how it turns out.

So the bias is never in the argument. The argument is usually rather good. That is what makes it such excellent camouflage.

The bias is in which argument you went looking for. And in how hard you looked for the other one.

Which is why this is nearly impossible to spot in yourself and blindingly obvious in the person across the table. Their case looks motivated.

Yours just looks like a case.

Kunda had a name for the state you end up in. She called it an "illusion of objectivity," a phrase she borrowed from Pyszczynski and Greenberg, which is a detail almost everyone gets wrong.

The illusion holds, she wrote, "because people do not realize that the process is biased by their goals, that they are accessing only a subset of their relevant knowledge, that they would probably access different beliefs and rules in the presence of different directional goals, and that they might even be capable of justifying opposite conclusions on different occasions."

It is the last part of that sentence that keeps you awake at two in the morning.

On a different day, with different things at stake, you could have argued the exact opposite. Just as sincerely. Just as well. With footnotes.

<div class="takeaway takeaway--rust"><span>Takeaway</span><p>You do not get to believe whatever you like. You only get to decide how hard the evidence has to work, and you set that price in private.</p></div>

## The evidence, briefly

In 1954, two researchers called Albert Hastorf and Hadley Cantril got there first, without having a name for it yet.

They showed students from Dartmouth and Princeton a film of a football game their two schools had played against each other. It had been a rough game. Everyone agreed it had been a rough game.

Then they asked the students to count the fouls.

The Dartmouth students watched their own side commit a handful. The Princeton students watched Dartmouth commit roughly twice as many.

Same film. Two completely different afternoons. Two completely honest sets of notes.

Twenty-five years later, Charles Lord, Lee Ross and Mark Lepper built the experiment everybody has been copying ever since.

They found 48 students. Half were in favour of the death penalty, half were against it, and all of them were quite sure the research was on their side. Each one read two studies about whether executions deter crime. One study said they do. One said they do not.

Here is the clever part. The researchers swapped the methods around, so the identical piece of research turned up for some students as proof the death penalty works, and for other students as proof it does not.

Both sides said the study that agreed with them was the better study.

Not more convincing. Better *built*. Same methods, opposite verdicts on those methods. The methodology section had apparently changed quality depending on who was reading it.

That finding is called biased assimilation and it has held up perfectly well ever since. The study's more famous claim did not. That was the idea that showing people balanced evidence drives them further apart, and it is best filed under unsettled. I will come back to why that distinction matters more than it looks.

### The experiment that settled it

Kunda ran her own study in 1987, and it is the cleanest thing in the whole literature. The part people leave out when they retell it is, of course, the part that counts.

Students read an article. It was presented as though it came from the science pages of *The New York Times*, and it said caffeine was dangerous. Three cups a day or more and you were looking at painful lumps in the breast tissue, the kind that worsen with age and that in their advanced stages are associated with breast cancer.

Dangerous if you were a woman, that is. The article was very specific about that.

The specificity was the whole design. A man who drinks four coffees a day and a woman who drinks four coffees a day believe roughly the same things about coffee. Only one of them has just been threatened. Kunda had been handed a control group that walked in pre-assembled.

Now. Here is what the women who drank a lot of coffee did not do.

They did not deny they were at risk. They rated their own chances of getting the disease as higher than the light drinkers did, and by a wide margin. No denial anywhere in the data.

What they did instead was quietly mark down the evidence. They were less persuaded by the link between caffeine and the disease. They were less persuaded by the biological explanation offered for it.

The men, who were in no danger whatsoever, found the science perfectly convincing.

Then Kunda ran a fourth study, and this is the one that closes the case. Identical procedure. She changed exactly one thing.

This time the disease was described as extremely common and entirely harmless, "so much so that doctors felt that it shouldn't even be considered a disease."

Beliefs about coffee: unchanged. Threat: gone.

The risk estimates came out the same as before. The scepticism evaporated. What little difference remained ran in the opposite direction and meant nothing at all.

Same information. Same prior beliefs. Different stakes.

Different verdict.

That is the experiment. Everything else in this post is commentary.

Kunda's own summary deserves far more attention than it gets, because the version of her theory that circulates at conferences is gloomier than anything she wrote.

*"It is clear that motivational forces do not completely blind people to undesirable evidence or information."*

People do take the bad news in. They are simply, in her words, not "as responsive as they might have been in the absence of motivational involvement."

So we are not blind.

We are just dimmer than we could be.

### The uncomfortable finding about experienced people

In 2006, Charles Taber and Milton Lodge ran the same kind of experiment on political argument. They opened their paper with Benjamin Franklin, who had the whole thing worked out two centuries early: *"So convenient a thing is it to be a rational creature, since it enables us to find or make a reason for everything one has a mind to."*

Their participants read arguments about contested policy. And they did three separate things with them.

They judged the agreeable arguments to be objectively stronger.

They spent longer arguing back at the ones they disagreed with, while letting the agreeable ones through without much thought at all.

And when they were allowed to choose what to read next, they chose the friendly sources.

None of that is surprising. This next bit is.

The bias was biggest among the people with the strongest views and the most expertise in the subject.

More knowledge is more ammunition. Which means that if you are the most experienced person in the room, you are not the most objective person in the room. You are simply the best equipped to build a respectable case for whatever you already wanted. Twenty years of pattern recognition, pointed at your own preferences.

To their credit, Taber and Lodge pointed at their own soft spots and admitted the status of selective exposure "can best be characterized as uncertain." The expertise finding has taken some real hits since, as you will see in a minute. Hold it loosely.

### The twist that explains what is really happening

If motivated reasoning just meant that wanting things makes you worse at thinking, then motivated people ought to fall apart on a hard logic problem.

In 2002, Erica Dawson, Thomas Gilovich and Dennis Regan tested that prediction the wrong way round on purpose, and what came back rearranges the whole picture.

They used the Wason selection task. It is one of those rare little puzzles where the correct move is to go hunting for evidence that would prove the rule *false*, which is precisely what almost nobody ever does. Then they varied one thing: whether you wanted the rule to be false.

For some participants the rule implied they would die young. For others it confirmed an unflattering stereotype about people like them.

Those participants, the threatened ones, "vastly outperformed" everybody else.

Threaten someone's self-image and they turn into a logician.

Their motivation did not damage their reasoning. It aimed it. And on this particular puzzle, it happened to aim it in exactly the right direction.

That is the sentence to carry out of here.

Motivated reasoning is not a shortage of thinking. It is rigour, unevenly aimed.

The colleague who can dismantle a proposal in ten minutes flat is not bad at analysis. She is superb at it.

The only question that has ever mattered is which proposal gets the ten minutes.

<div class="takeaway takeaway--blue"><span>Takeaway</span><p>Change the stakes and the same person, holding the same beliefs, reaches a different verdict. Before you question someone's judgement, check what the answer would cost them.</p></div>

## Where this shows up at work

A caveat first, because the rest of this post has been careful and this part should be too. Almost none of these experiments happened in an office. The mechanism is general. The settings were labs and ballot boxes. What follows is applied inference, not direct evidence.

So treat it as a list of places to look. Not as a finding.

**The business case that arrives after the decision.** You already know you want the acquisition, the re-platform, the reorg. The analysis that follows is genuine analysis, done in good faith, by capable people. It is also being marked by somebody who knows the answer they want. The tell is not a weak case. The case will be immaculate. The tell is that nobody was ever asked to build the other one.

**Hiring.** You form an impression in the first four minutes. Everything after that is evidence, waiting to be sorted. The candidate you liked has a gap in her CV because she was caring for a parent. The candidate you did not has a gap because he is unreliable. Both stories are perfectly plausible. Only one of them ever got told.

**Performance reviews.** Your strong performer had a rough quarter. New territory, a botched handover, a client who left for reasons nobody could have stopped. Your weak performer had a rough quarter, which fits the pattern. The context is real in both cases. You only went looking for it in one of them.

**Incident reviews.** The possible root cause that implicates the architecture you chose, or the deadline you set, or the hire you defended, gets the hardest cross-examination in the room. Nobody is covering anything up. You just happen to be the person in the building best equipped to explain why that particular cause does not quite fit.

**Metrics.** Numbers that back the roadmap are signal. Numbers that do not are noisy, lagging, seasonal, or measuring the wrong thing. Sometimes they genuinely are all four. The question is whether the same suspicion ever gets pointed the other way.

**Engagement surveys.** Bad results get a methodology review. Good results get a slide in the all-hands.

**Attrition.** Every departure arrives with a personal story attached. A partner relocating. A better offer. A life that changed shape. Every story is true.

Seven of them in a quarter is also a pattern, and you have a considerable interest in not noticing it.

{{< admonition type="warning" title="Four signs it is happening in the room" >}}
**The questions are one-sided.** Not that there are hard questions. That only one set of findings gets them.

**The standard of proof moves.** A result you like needs one supporting data point. A result you do not like needs a bigger sample, a longer window, a cleaner methodology.

**The alternative was never written down.** Not argued and rejected. Never drafted.

**Everyone feels fair.** This is the one that matters, and it is why none of the other three feel like warnings at the time.
{{< /admonition >}}

There is one more problem, and it gets worse the more authority you have.

Emily Pronin, Daniel Lin and Lee Ross found in 2002 that people rate themselves as less prone to bias than the average person. Less prone than their own classmates. Less prone than a stranger in an airport departure lounge.

Then it gets better, in the way that bad news gets better. Their participants "insisted that their self-assessments were accurate and objective **even after reading a description of how they could have been affected by the relevant bias**."

You can explain the bias to someone and they will agree it is a serious problem. For other people.

That part applies to everybody equally. It is not a failing of leaders.

The consequence is what does not apply equally. When a junior analyst's unexamined judgement is wrong, somebody catches it over coffee. When yours is wrong, it becomes the plan.

Here is why we cannot catch it ourselves. We look inward, we find no feeling of bias, and we reasonably conclude there is not any.

But motivated reasoning does not have a feeling. It happens one step earlier than the thinking you can watch yourself do. It happens in which considerations turned up in your head at all. By the time you sit down to weigh the evidence, the weighing may well be perfectly fair.

The choosing already happened.

So the loud confident voice saying *I am being objective about this* is not evidence of anything whatsoever.

It is the standard sensation. Everybody has it. Including the person you disagree with, who is currently having it about you.

<div class="takeaway"><span>Takeaway</span><p>If only one side of an argument ever gets audited, the audit is the bias. Stop checking how fair you feel and start counting which questions got asked.</p></div>

## The good news that got buried

Somewhere in the last decade this research escaped into the culture wearing a costume its authors never made for it.

*People cannot be reasoned with. Facts do not change minds. Everyone just believes what they want.*

That version is wrong. And it matters that it is wrong, because if you believe it you quietly stop bothering to bring evidence to your own team, which is a marvellous excuse dressed up as sophistication.

Here is what actually happened when the field went back and marked its own homework.

{{< admonition type="success" title="The short version" >}}
Motivated reasoning is real and replicates well in its core form. Several of the more dramatic claims built on top of it did not survive. And nearly all of the corrections point in the same direction: people are more persuadable than the slogan suggests.
{{< /admonition >}}

**The backfire effect is largely gone.** This is the big one.

In 2010, Brendan Nyhan and Jason Reifler reported that correcting a false belief could actually strengthen it in the very people you were trying to correct. The finding travelled fast and far. It became the evidence base for a decade of confident talk about how you simply cannot argue with facts.

Then Thomas Wood and Ethan Porter went looking for it properly. Five experiments. More than 10,100 people. Fifty-two issues, chosen precisely because backfire ought to show up there if it shows up anywhere.

They found none. Not a smaller effect. None.

Their conclusion: "By and large, citizens heed factual information, even when such information challenges their ideological commitments."

Nyhan wrote the correction himself, in *PNAS*, in 2021. Later work and press coverage, he said, had "seized on this finding, distorting its generality and exaggerating its role."

There is something quietly admirable in that. A man politely dismantling his own most famous result in public, while everyone else was still citing it.

The picture now is that corrections usually do work. They simply fade, and they get crowded out by more appealing claims. Fact-checking has a real measurable average effect. Modest, blunted by what people already believe, and positive.

Which means bringing inconvenient evidence to your team does not make things worse. That was never true. It just does not stick as well as you would like, which is a completely different problem and a far more solvable one.

**"Smarter people are more biased" is now contested.** In 2017, Dan Kahan and colleagues handed people a maths problem. Work out from a table of numbers whether a treatment worked.

Told it was a trial of a skin cream, the numerate participants did very well indeed.

Told it was a politically charged policy, accuracy collapsed and the answers lined up along political fault lines. The split "did not abate among subjects highest in numeracy; instead, it increased."

A striking result. It launched a thousand conference talks about how intelligence mainly makes you better at fooling yourself, a claim that flatters everyone in the audience and costs them nothing.

It has since failed two serious replication attempts. A preregistered one by Persson and colleagues in 2021, which "did not find good evidence for motivated numeracy." And a nationally representative sample by Stagnaro, Tappin and Rand in 2023, which found that more numerate people were *more* accurate regardless of their politics.

The exact opposite of the prediction.

Which takes some weight off the Taber and Lodge expertise finding from earlier. Hold that one loosely too.

**Some of what looks like belief is not belief at all.** In 2015, Bullock, Gerber, Hill and Huber tried paying people small sums for correct answers, and small sums for honestly admitting they did not know. Doing that "sharply diminish[es] the gap between Democrats and Republicans" on questions of plain fact.

Prior, Sood and Khanna found the same thing that year without spending a penny, using nothing more than a line on the screen asking people to be accurate.

Many people, they concluded, "interpret factual questions about economic conditions as opinion questions, unless motivated to see them otherwise."

Sit with that. If a small incentive closes most of a gap, then a good deal of what looked like distorted belief was closer to cheering for a team. The gaps shrink without disappearing, and what remains is still argued over. But we have been overestimating how deep this goes.

The translation for your Tuesday morning: when somebody gives you an answer that seems stubbornly, almost wilfully wrong, ask whether the question felt to them like a loyalty test. If it did, changing what the question signals will do more than any amount of evidence ever will.

**Disagreement is not proof of bias.** The most underrated idea in the entire literature, demonstrated formally by John Bullock in 2009:

> "Learning need not create agreement among Bayesians. **Disagreement among partisans is never clear evidence of bias.**"

If two people rate the *source* differently, the very same evidence can push them apart while both of them reason impeccably.

Druckman and McGrath argued in 2019 that much of the climate-opinion data is "equally consistent with a theory in which citizens strive to form accurate beliefs but vary in what they consider to be credible evidence." Tappin, Pennycook and Rand tested this experimentally and found "little evidence that analytic thinking magnified politically biased deviations" from a rational benchmark, and consistent evidence that thinking harder moved people closer to it.

This one is genuinely useful for a manager, because it means your instinct is frequently wrong.

When two capable people read the same report and walk out with opposite conclusions, the explanation is very often not that one of them is motivated. They trust the source differently. Or one of them knows something you do not about how that data gets collected.

Accusing somebody of bias when all they have is different information is an expensive mistake. It is also the exact mistake this literature makes easiest to commit, which is a fine irony for a body of work about jumping to convenient conclusions.

**People update more than the folklore says.** Seth Hill went and measured it in 2017. People "learn as cautious Bayesians, updating their beliefs at about **73% of perfect application of Bayes' Rule**."

Biased, certainly. Welcome information got absorbed almost perfectly, unwelcome information fell short.

But 73% is nowhere near immovable. Beliefs drifted toward the truth, and they drifted faster with a small nudge in the direction of accuracy.

Tappin, Berinsky and Rand ran the large version in 2023. Four and a half thousand people, twenty-four policy issues, and messages deliberately contradicted by a leader those people trusted. The leader's cue moved opinion, as you would expect. But there was "no evidence that the cues meaningfully diminished partisans' receptivity to the messages."

People took in both. And weighed both.

Which is about the most reassuring thing you could know before your next difficult conversation. The evidence you bring will land.

It will simply be one voice among several in the room. And it will need saying more than once.

<div class="takeaway takeaway--rust"><span>Takeaway</span><p>Facts do change minds. They just work slowly, fade quietly, and need repeating. Bring the evidence anyway, then bring it again.</p></div>

## Why it happens

Put the contested claims aside and the machinery underneath is reasonably well understood. Four moving parts.

**The search is one-sided.** Kunda's central proposal. People "search memory for those beliefs and rules that could support their desired conclusion," and they will "creatively combine accessed knowledge to construct new beliefs." Whatever comes back from that search then gets judged perfectly fairly.

The damage was done before the judging started. The jury is scrupulous. Somebody else picked the witnesses.

**The bar moves.** *Can I* against *Must I*, in practice. Ditto and Lopez showed you need less evidence to accept a conclusion you want than one you do not.

Not different evidence. Less of it.

**The examination is uneven.** This is not distorted conclusions drawn from equal scrutiny. It is unequal scrutiny. More retesting. Longer deliberation. More excuses generated. A later decision to stop looking.

The office version is the meeting at the top of this page.

**We choose what we look at.** Hart and colleagues, in a 2009 meta-analysis, found a genuine preference for agreeable information. The interesting part is what moved it. Defensiveness made it bigger. A goal of accuracy made it smaller. And the preference reversed outright when the unwelcome information turned out to be useful for something the person was trying to do.

That is Kunda's two goals, confirmed across an entire literature. It also means none of this is welded in place. It responds to what you are genuinely trying to achieve.

Underneath all four sits feeling.

Taber and Lodge build their model on automatic emotional response. Munro and Ditto found in 1997 that negative emotion carried the biased processing along. And Kahan describes the whole thing in a way any leader will recognise instantly as a structural problem rather than a personal failing: "a distinctive conflict of interest: between the personal interest individuals have in forming beliefs in line with those held by others with whom they share close ties and the collective one they all share in making use of the best available science."

Swap "close ties" for "team" and "the collective" for "the company" and there it is, on your org chart.

Somebody on your team has a real personal stake in believing what their group believes. That stake is not imaginary and it is not weakness. And telling them to be more rational does precisely nothing about it, because you have addressed the belief and left the incentive exactly where it was.

{{< admonition type="abstract" title="A note on the brain-scan studies, since they get abused" open=false >}}
Westen and colleagues (2006) scanned 30 committed partisans during the 2004 US election and found activation in emotion-related regions, and *not* in regions associated with cold reasoning. With *n* = 30, and given the well-known limits of inferring what someone is thinking from which bit of the brain lights up (Poldrack, 2006), this is *consistent with* an emotional account rather than proof of one.

The paper does not say the reasoning centres "shut down." The much-repeated line about partisan brains lighting up like an addict getting a fix comes from press commentary, not the article.
{{< /admonition >}}

**And we cannot see any of it.** This came up earlier, but it belongs on any list of mechanisms. Pronin, Lin and Ross showed that looking inward does not catch this, for the simple reason that there is nothing in there to catch. The selecting happens before the thinking you can watch yourself do.

<div class="takeaway takeaway--blue"><span>Takeaway</span><p>The bias is in the search, not the verdict. So fix what gets put in front of the room, not how fair-minded everyone promises to be.</p></div>

Which brings us to the single most useful finding in the whole literature.

## What actually works

### Telling people to be objective does not work

This is the bit worth the price of admission, and very nearly every organisation on earth gets it wrong.

In 1984, Lord, Lepper and Preston put two debiasing instructions head to head.

The first asked people to be "as fair and unbiased as possible."

The second asked them to consider the opposite. Specifically: would you have judged this study the same way if it had come out the other way round?

Consider-the-opposite won. The appeal to fairness, which the authors drily called "more demand-laden," did comparatively little.

The reason is the blind spot from earlier. Telling a motivated reasoner to be objective is asking them to want something they already sincerely believe they have. You handed them a goal. What they needed was a method.

Consider-the-opposite hands them an operation. It drags the missing half of the evidence into the room, which is exactly where the bias had been sitting all along, quietly, with its coat still on.

Every "we value intellectual honesty" slide is the first instruction.

Every pre-mortem is the second.

One of them is printed on a mug. The other one actually works.

Hirt and Markman added a useful refinement in 1995. It is not opposition that does the work. It is breaking out of a single-hypothesis mindset. Any plausible alternative will do.

And there is one boundary condition that matters enormously in practice. Asking for alternatives that are hard to generate can backfire, because the struggle itself gets quietly read as evidence that you were right all along.

Ask someone for ten reasons the plan might fail and they will walk away more confident than when they sat down, having found reasons eight, nine and ten a bit of a stretch.

So ask for one. Maybe two.

{{< admonition type="tip" title="The one-sentence version" >}}
Not *"let me be fair about this."*

Instead: **"If this had come out the other way, would I be scrutinising it this hard?"**

Then actually answer it. Out loud, if you can bear it.
{{< /admonition >}}

### A practical version for teams

Every one of these is a direct application of something above. And none of them asks anybody to be more virtuous than they already are, which is rather the point.

**Write the opposing case before the decision, not after.** Give somebody the job of building the strongest version of the option you are not taking, while the outcome is still genuinely open. Not a devil's advocate performance at the end, when everyone knows the decision is made and the whole thing is dinner theatre.

**Ask for one strong objection. Not a list.** Remember Hirt and Markman. A long list of weak objections leaves people more confident than they started. "What is the single best argument against this?" beats "what are the risks?" every time.

**Decide who reviews the reasoning before the reasoning starts.** Lerner and Tetlock reviewed this in 1999 and found accountability is conditional, not a general cure. Being accountable to an audience whose views you already know produces conformity, because people write toward the boss. Being accountable to an audience you do not know, and knowing that before you form a judgement, produces real self-criticism.

Only the second one helps. So name the reviewer early, and do not broadcast what they think.

**Say the accuracy goal out loud.** It sounds far too simple to work. Prior, Sood and Khanna got measurable improvement from nothing more than a line on a screen. Pennycook and colleagues showed in 2021 that nudging attention toward accuracy improves the quality of what people go on to share.

Opening a review with "we are here to find out what is true, not to defend the plan" is close to free. A genuine intervention rather than a platitude, provided the rest of the meeting does not immediately contradict it.

**Separate the diagnosis from the consequence.** The most structural fix on the list, and easily the most neglected.

Campbell and Kay showed in 2014 that people reject a factual claim partly because they reject the *solution* it implies. They found it running in both political directions, so it is not a quirk of one worldview.

The office version is everywhere. If admitting the project is behind means somebody loses their job, then nobody will be able to see that the project is behind.

Not will not. Will not be *able to*.

You have made the fact expensive, and motivated reasoning is superb at making expensive facts look doubtful. If you want honest status reporting, lower the price first.

**Make it safe to be wrong without being made small.** Self-affirmation, which means having people think about something they value in themselves before they face threatening evidence, has decent theory behind it. Cohen, Aronson and Steele found in 2000 that affirmed participants were more moved by evidence against their own position, and, rather wonderfully, more critical of the people who agreed with them.

Be honest about the size of it though. Two independent meta-analyses in 2015 found small effects, with lower confidence bounds close to zero on some outcomes, and both were about health messages rather than boardrooms.

Take it as a good reason to start hard conversations by acknowledging what someone has done well. Not as a technique with a guaranteed return.

**Consider that it might not be motivation at all.** Pennycook and Rand tested 3,446 people in 2019 and found the ones who thought more carefully were *better* at telling true headlines from false ones, "even for headlines that aligned with individuals' political ideology." Falling for misinformation, they concluded, is "driven more by lazy thinking than it is by partisan bias per se."

So before you diagnose a deep motivational problem on your team, check the boring explanation.

Sometimes people did not defend a bad conclusion because they were invested in it. Sometimes nobody had a spare hour to go and look.

**And here is where the evidence runs out,** so you know. Perspective-taking and intellectual-humility training as specific remedies have not been established. Neither has the durability of debiasing programmes, or whether they survive outside the room where they were taught. Julia Galef's scout and soldier framing is genuinely useful for thinking with, but it is a trade book rather than an evidence base, and deserves to be cited as one.

<div class="takeaway"><span>Takeaway</span><p>Do not ask anyone to be fairer. Ask one question they cannot dodge, name the reviewer before the work starts, and make the inconvenient answer cheap to say.</p></div>

## It is not just meetings

Politics dominates the research because polarisation is easy to measure and easy to get funded. The thing itself is not political.

**Feedback about yourself.** Eil and Rao gave people objective information about their own intelligence and their own attractiveness in 2011. The good news went in almost rationally. The bad news produced erratic updating and, this is the part worth noticing, an active reluctance to find out any more. A neutral control showed no asymmetry at all. And because their design pulled apart wanting something from expecting it, they could show the asymmetry "is driven by direction; confirmation alone had no effect."

If you have ever wondered why 360 feedback so reliably changes nothing, start there. The unflattering half does not get rejected. It gets doubted, and then it never gets followed up.

**Medicine.** Ditto and Lopez's students were doing exactly what patients do. Asking for a second opinion, but only sometimes. Questioning the accuracy of unwelcome results. Finding excellent reasons the test might be wrong.

On the other side of the desk, Croskerry and colleagues documented in 2013 how clinicians close prematurely on a favourite diagnosis, though they put most of it down to fast automatic thinking rather than motivation. A useful reminder that not every bias is a hot one.

**Law.** Avani Sood's 2013 review is the way in, and the sharpest point in it is this. Formal safeguards, like instructing a jury to disregard evidence or to be impartial, are structurally the same kind of general instruction that Lord, Lepper and Preston found worked less well than a concrete procedure.

Courts, like companies, mostly use the version that does not work. At least the courts have the excuse of precedent.

**Money, with real stakes.** Meeuwis and colleagues found in 2022 that after the 2016 US election, investors likely to be Republicans increased their exposure to equities while likely Democrats moved into safer assets, with other explanations controlled for.

These were people's actual portfolios. Their actual money. Though the authors' own scoping is worth quoting: the findings "are driven by a small share of investors making big changes."

**Relationships.** Gagné and Lydon found in 2004 that people run both modes and switch between them. Positively biased and frankly not very accurate while they are pursuing a relationship. Then noticeably more accurate when a real decision point arrives. Sometimes getting more accurate on the factual judgements while staying warmly, generously biased on the esteem judgements, in the very same moment.

Which is to say you already own the switch. You have been flicking it for years, at home, in private, without ever putting it on a slide.

That is the most hopeful finding in this entire post, and I will come back to it in a second.

<div class="takeaway takeaway--rust"><span>Takeaway</span><p>You already switch between defending a conclusion and testing one. You just do it in your personal life, on instinct, and leave the skill at home on weekday mornings.</p></div>

## The reasonable conclusion

Two findings deserve the last word, because both of them cut against the gloom this subject invites.

The first belongs to Redlawsk, Civettini and Emmerson, who in 2010 went looking for the point where a motivated reasoner finally gives way.

They found one.

Pile up enough disconfirming evidence and people do update. They called it an affective tipping point. Anxiety climbs as you get near it, which suggests the thing that eventually forces accuracy on us is not cold logic at all. It is discomfort.

Their conclusion: voters "are not immune to disconfirming information after all, even when initially acting as motivated reasoners."

Two things follow, for anyone trying to change a mind, including their own.

Persistence is not futile. The resistance you meet early is not the final answer, and the evidence is stacking up even when it looks like absolutely nothing is happening.

And the discomfort people show on the way there is not a sign your approach is failing. It is quite often the sign that it is working.

The second finding is Gagné and Lydon's, which is really a claim about what people are capable of.

We already know how to switch between defending a position and testing one. We do it in our marriages. Constantly. Without training, without a framework, usually without noticing.

So the skill is not missing.

Nobody ever pointed it at the quarterly review.

Which leaves the honest summary of thirty-five years of research sounding narrower than the slogan, and a great deal more useful. Motivation does not make us incapable of reasoning. It sets the price of admission for a conclusion, and it sets that price differently depending on whether we would like the conclusion to be true.

We are not blind. We are, as Kunda put it, simply not as responsive as we might have been.

And the most reliable thing you can do about any of it costs one sentence and about ten seconds of honest discomfort:

*If this had come out the other way, would I be looking at it this hard?*

Ask it in your next meeting. Then brace yourself, because the first honest answer is usually no.
