In 1992, psychologists [Peter Ditto and David Lopez](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.63.4.568) asked students to rub a strip of treated paper in their saliva. They were told it tested for an enzyme linked to a risk of pancreatic problems.

Half were told a colour change meant bad news. The other half were told that no colour change meant bad news.

The strip was a fake. It never changed colour. Every student watched exactly the same nothing happen, but only some of them liked what it meant.

The ones who got the bad news watched longer. Fifty-two percent tested themselves again, compared with eighteen percent of those told they were fine. Often they used the very same strip, as though the enzyme might have reconsidered.

They also questioned the test's accuracy. Perhaps they had slept badly or eaten something strange. Perhaps the test was not particularly reliable in the first place.

Somewhere in that room, a student with a wet piece of paper invented peer review.

They did not need to reject the result outright. They just checked it more carefully than the people who liked what they got.

That uneven standard is one way **motivated reasoning** shows up: the answer we want changes how hard we question the evidence. It does not require dishonesty. It can feel exactly like being sensible.

## Your Reasoning Has an Agenda

Thomas Gilovich captured the distinction in a question:

> "For desired conclusions, in other words, it is as if we ask ourselves, 'Can I believe this?', but for unpalatable conclusions we ask, 'Must I believe this?'"

*Can I* is an easy question. One decent reason may settle it. *Must I* is harder. Any flaw, alternative explanation, or complaint about the sample size gives you permission to set the answer down for a while.

The tricky part is that the objections can be legitimate. A small sample really is a problem. A source can be unreliable. Motivated reasoning does not require you to invent a bad argument. It can work by making you unusually resourceful at finding a good one on only one side.

In her influential [1990 review](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.108.3.480), psychologist Ziva Kunda described how our goals can shape what we remember, which explanations we consider, and when we decide we have enough evidence.

Sometimes the goal is accuracy: we want the answer that best fits the facts. Sometimes we want a particular answer, perhaps because it protects our confidence, our plans, or our place in a group. Those goals can coexist. Sincerely wanting to be fair does not mean you have stopped caring how the result turns out.

Kunda's important qualification was that we cannot simply believe anything we please. We usually need a justification that seems capable of persuading someone else. The bias can begin before we assess the argument, in which evidence we bother to collect.

Consider the difference between asking, "Is this a good decision?" and asking, "What are the reasons this decision could work?" Both can produce thoughtful answers. Only one has left the outcome properly open.

This overlaps with **confirmation bias**, the tendency to favour information that fits what we already believe. The useful distinction is between what we *believe* and what we *want* to be true. They often coincide, but they are not the same thing.

That is also why feeling objective is a poor test of whether you are. You can inspect the reasons in front of you and find them convincing without noticing the reasons you never went looking for.

The check is not whether you feel fair. It is whether a conclusion you dislike has to clear hurdles that a welcome conclusion does not.

## Same Coffee. Different Stakes.

An earlier [experiment by Kunda](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.53.4.636) makes the distinction easier to see.

Students read an article presented as a newspaper science report. It claimed that heavy caffeine consumption increased the risk of a breast condition, described in a threatening way. This was a claim participants were asked to evaluate, not a medical conclusion about coffee established by the experiment.

The researchers could compare responses according to coffee consumption and whether the described health threat applied to the participant. The article gave heavy coffee-drinking women a personal reason to hope it was wrong that it did not give the men.

What happened was subtler than simple denial. Women who drank more coffee still rated their own risk as higher than light drinkers did. They had taken in the unwelcome information. But they were less persuaded by the proposed caffeine link and its explanation.

In other words, "This might affect me" and "I am not convinced by this evidence" could exist in the same head. The result did not have to be ignored for the scrutiny to become uneven.

In a later study, the condition was described as common and harmless. The earlier pattern of scepticism was no longer apparent. That comparison supported a role for personal stakes, rather than treating every difference as simply a disagreement about coffee.

There are limits. These were particular studies with particular participants, not a demonstration of what every coffee drinker thinks. A difference that is not statistically detectable does not prove two groups are identical. Nor does this design settle every question about how prior beliefs and motivation interact.

Still, the distinction is useful. We can acknowledge bad news while giving ourselves additional reasons not to act on it yet. We can recognise a possibility while demanding an unusually high standard of proof before accepting it.

That extra scrutiny might find a real weakness. It might even lead to the right answer. The concern is not that an unwanted conclusion must be correct. It is that the process for evaluating it has quietly changed.

You do not need to catch yourself thinking, "I refuse to believe this." A more familiar sentence is, "I would just like a little more evidence."

Sometimes that is exactly the right request. The question is whether you would have made it if the evidence had been kinder to you.

## Same Habit. Different Settings.

Most of the research described here comes from controlled tasks and political or personal judgements. The following are everyday illustrations of the mechanism, not diagnoses or findings from those experiments.

**The meeting.** A project the team has backed for months comes back with disappointing results. Was the sample big enough? Did it run over the holidays? Was the tracking set up properly?

Every question is reasonable. But last month's encouraging result, gathered the same way, received none of them. It went straight into the presentation.

They are being rigorous. They are just not being rigorous evenly.

**The expensive purchase.** You buy a new phone, then read that its battery life is poor. The reviewer probably had a faulty unit. Their testing was unrealistic. They have always preferred the other brand.

A favourable review arrives and suddenly none of those checks seem urgent. You do not need to be lying about the phone. You may just be more interested in reassurance now that the money has left your account.

**The feedback.** A friend says you interrupt them. You can immediately remember three occasions when you listened patiently, and several when they interrupted you. You build a perfectly plausible case that the criticism is unfair.

When someone calls you a good listener, however, you do not request dates, examples, and an independent witness.

The friend might be wrong. The phone reviewer might be careless. The project might deserve more time. None of these situations requires accepting every criticism or giving weak evidence the same weight as strong evidence.

The useful comparison is between your standards. Do you check favourable reviews too? Did you agree on the project's success criteria before seeing the numbers? Can you ask your friend for an example without treating the answer as a case to defeat?

Notice how quickly this can become a theory about other people. The colleague with the pet project. The friend who cannot take criticism. The stranger defending a politician.

It is a much more useful theory when it interrupts one of your own sentences.

## Fairness Needs a Method

Telling yourself to "be objective" leaves a practical problem: you probably already think you are. A specific procedure gives you something to do when that feeling is not a reliable guide.

In experiments by [Lord, Lepper and Preston](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.47.6.1231), asking people to consider the opposite helped reduce bias more than a general appeal to be fair. That does not make it a universal cure. It does suggest a better starting point than another promise to keep an open mind.

Here are three ways to put that into an ordinary decision or conversation.

### 1. Reverse the result, then inspect your questions

Ask: **"If this had come out the other way, would I be scrutinising it this hard?"**

Keep everything else fixed. Same source. Same sample. Same method. Only the conclusion changes. Would you still be asking for more data, questioning the author's motives, or postponing the decision?

Write down the answer before explaining it away. If the standard changes, you have found something worth examining. You have not proved the conclusion you dislike is correct. You have identified a reason to revisit how you judged it.

For decisions you can anticipate, make the comparison easier by agreeing on the standard in advance. What would count as success? What result would make you reconsider? Which sources would you trust either way?

That is useful before a project review, but also before a purchase. Decide which features matter before you fall in love with the product. Otherwise the feature it happens to excel at can become, retrospectively, the one you cared about all along.

### 2. Find one strong objection, not ten weak ones

Ask: **"What is the best reason my preferred explanation might be wrong?"**

The aim is not to perform an argument with an imaginary opponent who conveniently loses. Find an alternative that a thoughtful person could actually hold, and give it a fair account.

Research on considering alternatives suggests that generating a plausible different explanation can loosen commitment to the first one. But struggling to produce a long list can have the opposite effect: the difficulty starts to feel like evidence that your original view must be sound.

One good objection is enough to begin. Ask what evidence would distinguish it from your explanation, then look for that evidence. If you cannot find it, name the uncertainty instead of treating the absence as a victory.

With other people, try summarising their strongest point before replying. Ask whether you have understood it correctly. You are not promising agreement; you are checking that you are disagreeing with the argument they made, rather than the easier version you prepared while they were talking.

### 3. Make changing your mind less costly

Ask: **"What would admitting this mean for me, or for the person I am asking?"**

An unwelcome fact may arrive bundled with an unwelcome consequence: wasted money, an apology, lost status, or a difficult conversation. Research on solution aversion suggests that disliking a proposed response can affect whether people accept the problem itself.

Separate the two where you can. First ask what the evidence says. Then discuss what to do about it. Accepting that the phone has a weakness does not mean every reason you bought it was foolish. Admitting that you interrupted someone does not mean you never listen.

In a team, this also means taking your own behaviour seriously. If people who report problems get blamed, asking for honest updates will not undo the incentive to defend the plan. At home, an admission followed by a triumphant "I told you so" is unlikely to make the next admission easier.

Try making a revision ordinary: "That made sense with what I knew then. This changes my view." Leave room for the other person to do the same without turning the conversation into a verdict on their character.

These are practices, not guarantees. They work on different parts of the problem: the standard of proof, the evidence considered, and the cost of accepting the answer. You can use one without pretending it has made you immune to the others.

## The good news that got buried

It is tempting to leave this subject with a gloomy conclusion: people believe what they want, so there is no point bringing them evidence. That goes further than the research warrants. Three qualifications matter before we start diagnosing everyone at dinner.

**Corrections generally help.** Early studies of a "backfire effect" raised the possibility that correcting a false belief could strengthen it. Later large studies, including work by [Wood and Porter](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11109-018-9443-y), did not find that to be a reliable general pattern.

That does not mean every correction works, lasts, or changes behaviour. Trust, attention, repetition, and the surrounding conversation still matter. But resistance is not a reason to assume facts are useless, or that presenting them will normally make someone more mistaken. A correction can improve a belief without resolving every disagreement attached to it.

**Experience is not immunity. But thinking is not the enemy.** Some studies found stronger bias among more knowledgeable participants. Other findings, including prominent claims about numerical ability making political bias worse, have been challenged by replication work.

The sensible conclusion is narrower than "smart people are better at fooling themselves." Knowledge can help us defend a preferred answer, and it can help us recognise that the answer is wrong. Careful thinking remains valuable. What needs checking is how we direct it, not whether we should bother doing it.

That also means you should not discount someone's expertise merely because they care about the outcome. Evaluate their reasons and the process used to reach them.

**Disagreement is not proof of bias.** Two people can reason carefully from different starting information. They may trust different sources, have different experiences, or interpret the reliability of a measurement differently. An outcome that looks obvious to you may depend on assumptions they do not share.

Before deciding someone is defending a belief because they need it to be true, ask what they know and why they trust it. Ask the same of yourself. The framework is not a shortcut around that conversation.

These qualifications are not small print that ruins the idea. They keep it useful. Without them, "motivated reasoning" becomes another convenient explanation for why everyone who disagrees with us is doing thinking incorrectly. That would be a rather unfortunate use of this particular lesson.

## One Question Before the Next Meeting

Or the next purchase. Or the next conversation you have already decided to win.

You do not have to become a person with no preferences. You have to notice when a preference starts setting the standard of proof. That can happen while you are asking intelligent questions, making valid objections, and feeling entirely fair.

Before asking for one more test, one more review, or one more example, pause:

*If this had come out the other way, would I be looking at it this hard?*

If the answer is no, you need not leap to the opposite conclusion. Give the neglected evidence a hearing. Apply the same checks to the answer you wanted. Leave yourself room to revise.

The goal is not to lose the argument gracefully. It is to find out whether you should still be making it.
