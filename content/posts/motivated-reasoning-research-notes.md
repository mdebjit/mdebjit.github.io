## The theoretical distinction

In her 1990 review, Ziva Kunda distinguished goals directed at accuracy from goals directed at reaching a preferred conclusion, crediting earlier work by Arie Kruglanski. Her account concerns how motivation can affect the memories, beliefs, and reasoning strategies available for constructing a conclusion.

It does not require an ability to believe anything at will. People generally seek a justification that appears rational and could persuade a dispassionate observer. A plausible argument can therefore coexist with a biased search for supporting material. This does not mean the argument itself is always sound or unaffected by motivation.

Kunda described an "illusion of objectivity," drawing on Pyszczynski and Greenberg: people may not recognise that a different goal would have brought different considerations to mind. Introspection alone may miss that selection process.

Several neighbouring concepts are useful, but they should not become interchangeable labels:

- **Confirmation bias:** favouring evidence that supports an existing belief. The relationship between prior belief and desired outcome needs to be examined rather than assumed.
- **Cognitive dissonance:** discomfort associated with inconsistent beliefs or behaviour, which can motivate attempts to resolve the inconsistency. It is not a synonym for every instance of motivated reasoning.
- **Rationalisation:** constructing a justification, often after a decision. It describes a possible output rather than a complete account of the process.
- **Motivated scepticism:** evaluating unwelcome evidence more critically than welcome evidence.
- **Identity-protective cognition:** reasoning associated with maintaining commitments or standing within a valued group. Kahan's work emphasises the social incentives involved.

The broad empirical challenge is to distinguish a desire for a conclusion from differences in prior information, trust, or interpretation. Observing disagreement does not by itself make that distinction.

## Additional experiments

### Seeing the same game differently

Hastorf and Cantril (1954) asked Dartmouth and Princeton students to evaluate a film of a game between the two schools. Their reports differed substantially, including how many infractions they attributed to Dartmouth.

The study is a memorable illustration of selective perception. It should not be treated as a design that cleanly isolates every possible difference in motivation, prior beliefs, and group identification.

### Biased assimilation and polarisation

Lord, Ross and Lepper (1979) presented people with opposing views about capital punishment with research pointing in different directions. By varying which methods supported which conclusion, the study examined whether evaluations of research quality followed participants' existing positions.

Two claims need separating: rating agreeable evidence more favourably, and becoming more polarised after encountering balanced evidence. Evidence for biased evaluation does not automatically establish that balanced information generally drives people further apart. The polarisation claim has a more contested history.

### Motivated scepticism about a health test

Ditto and Lopez (1992) used a supposed saliva test whose implications varied across participants. Those receiving an unwelcome result scrutinised it more and were more likely to test themselves again. The longer draft recorded retesting rates of 52% versus 18%.

The finding concerns how scrutiny changes with the desirability of an outcome. It is not a reason to regard seeking a second medical opinion as evidence of bias. Real tests differ in reliability and consequences, and follow-up testing may be appropriate.

### Coffee, personal risk, and scepticism

Kunda (1987) examined reactions to a purported article associating caffeine consumption with a breast condition. In the threatening version, women who drank more coffee acknowledged greater personal risk while expressing less conviction about the proposed link and mechanism.

The design distinguished risk judgements from judgements about the evidence. These are not the same outcome. A participant can accept that a claim, if true, matters to them while being less persuaded that the claim is well supported.

A later study described the condition as common and harmless. The earlier conviction pattern was not apparent. This comparison supports investigating personal stakes, but a nonsignificant contrast does not prove that motivation had no effect, or that groups were equivalent. Comparing a significant result in one study with a nonsignificant result in another is not, by itself, a statistical test of the difference between them.

The health claim was material used in a reasoning experiment. This paper is not evidence that coffee causes the described condition.

### When motivation improves a reasoning task

Dawson, Gilovich and Regan (2002) used versions of the Wason selection task. Participants motivated to disprove a threatening rule performed better in these tasks, where searching for disconfirming evidence was the appropriate strategy.

The finding complicates the idea that motivation simply reduces reasoning ability. In this setting, the desired outcome encouraged a useful strategy. It does not show that threatening people generally improves their judgement.

### Political expertise and selective scrutiny

Taber and Lodge (2006) examined evaluations of political arguments, counterargument, and selective exposure. Their results included stronger bias among participants with stronger attitudes and greater political sophistication.

Political sophistication is not interchangeable with intelligence, numerical ability, or expertise in every domain. Later challenges to motivated-numeracy findings also are not direct replications of every claim in this paper. The defensible general point is that expertise does not guarantee impartiality, not that knowledgeable people are necessarily less accurate.

### The bias blind spot

Pronin, Lin and Ross (2002) examined people's tendency to see themselves as less susceptible to bias than others. Participants could retain confidence in their own objectivity even after learning about a relevant bias.

This gives a reason to inspect decision procedures instead of relying only on a feeling of fairness. It does not establish that self-reflection is useless or that people can never recognise their own motivations.

## Reading the statistics correctly

**A p-value is conditional on a model.** It is the probability, under the specified null hypothesis and statistical assumptions, of obtaining a test statistic at least as extreme as the observed one. It is not the probability that the null hypothesis is true, that a finding is false, or that the result was "just a fluke."

A small p-value is not an effect size or a guarantee that a result will replicate. A large one does not prove there is no effect. Evidence that an effect is negligibly small requires an appropriate analysis, such as an equivalence test with a justified margin, rather than simply failing to reject a null hypothesis.

**Test statistics are not interchangeable measures of the size of an effect.** F, t, and chi-squared statistics have different reference distributions and depend on quantities such as variability and sample size. Degrees of freedom and whether a test is one-sided or two-sided matter when interpreting a reported p-value.

**An interaction concerns a difference in effects.** In the coffee study, the question is whether the relationship with consumption differs by sex. A significant result within one subgroup and a nonsignificant result within another are not sufficient on their own to establish an interaction.

### Effect sizes and pooled findings

Correlation r and standardised mean difference d describe different quantities. Neither should be read as a percentage of people affected. The familiar small, medium, and large benchmarks are rough conventions, not context-free measures of importance.

| Research | Values retained from the longer draft | Scope to preserve |
|---|---|---|

Uncertainty intervals and differences among study designs belong alongside effect estimates in a publishable version. A row of point estimates cannot establish the strength or generality of an entire theory.

## Corrections and contested claims

**Backfire is not a reliable general rule.** Nyhan and Reifler (2010) reported circumstances in which corrections strengthened misperceptions. Wood and Porter (2019) subsequently investigated a much larger set of issues and participants without finding the expected general backfire pattern. Swire-Thompson, DeGutis and Lazer (2020) reviewed methodological concerns, and Nyhan (2021) cautioned against exaggerating the original finding's generality.

The resulting practical point is that corrections often improve factual accuracy. It is not that every correction works, or that belief change necessarily persists or changes behaviour.

**Motivated numeracy is contested.** Kahan and colleagues (2017) reported that political framing changed performance on a numerical reasoning problem, with polarisation increasing among highly numerate participants. Persson and colleagues (2021) and Stagnaro, Tappin and Rand (2023) challenged this pattern in later studies. Differences in design and sampling matter. These results should not be compressed into a universal rule about intelligence making people more biased.

**Expressive answers are not necessarily sincere beliefs.** Bullock, Gerber, Hill and Huber (2015) used incentives for accuracy and acknowledging uncertainty. Prior, Sood and Khanna (2015) examined accuracy motivation. Reductions in partisan answer gaps suggest that some survey responses express affiliation as well as belief. They do not establish that all disagreement is performative.

**Different starting points can produce disagreement without irrationality.** Bullock (2009), Gerber and Green (1999), and Druckman and McGrath (2019) highlight problems with inferring bias without accounting for prior beliefs and source credibility. Tappin, Pennycook and Rand examined departures from Bayesian benchmarks and the role of analytic thinking. An appropriate benchmark and a clear account of participants' information are essential.

**Updating and social cues can coexist.** Hill (2017) studied belief updating, and Tappin, Berinsky and Rand (2023) examined persuasive messages alongside countervailing party-leader cues. People can respond to both evidence and social signals. Neither complete independence nor complete resistance should be assumed.

## Further work on changing the process

**Considering alternatives.** Lord, Lepper and Preston (1984) compared considering the opposite with general instructions to be fair. Hirt and Markman (1995) investigated generating alternatives and the experience of doing so. The practical question is how to make a plausible alternative available, not how to demand an arbitrarily long list of objections.

**Accountability.** Lerner and Tetlock (1999) reviewed conditions under which accountability changes judgement. Being answerable to an audience is not automatically a cure: people may adapt their reasoning to an audience whose preferred answer they know. Timing and audience expectations matter.

**Accuracy prompts.** Prior, Sood and Khanna (2015) and Pennycook and colleagues (2021) offer relevant work on drawing attention to accuracy. Survey answers and decisions about sharing headlines are specific outcomes. An accuracy prompt in a team meeting is an application to test, not an established substitute for evidence or incentives.

**Solution aversion.** Campbell and Kay (2014) examined how reactions to proposed solutions can affect acceptance of a problem. Separating a factual assessment from an argument about what must follow from it may be useful. That does not mean the consequences of accepting a fact can always be removed.

**Self-affirmation.** Cohen, Aronson and Steele (2000) studied responses to evidence after self-affirmation. Reviews by Epton and colleagues and Sweeney and Moyer (2015) considered health-related outcomes. Effects were limited, and uncertainty matters. Acknowledging someone's contributions in a difficult conversation is not identical to the experimental interventions.

**Effort rather than motivation.** Pennycook and Rand (2019) found that analytic thinking was associated with better discrimination between true and false headlines, including politically congenial ones. Before attributing an error to a desired conclusion, consider insufficient attention, missing information, and task difficulty.

The duration of improvements and transfer to new settings remain separate questions. A successful laboratory instruction does not establish a lasting change in everyday reasoning. Julia Galef's scout and soldier framing is a useful popular explanation, not itself an experimental evidence base.

## Evidence outside the main narrative

- **Personal feedback:** Eil and Rao (2011) studied responses to information about intelligence and attractiveness, distinguishing desirable outcomes from expected ones. The asymmetry they reported is relevant to self-image, but does not establish that all resistance to feedback is motivational.
- **Clinical judgement:** Croskerry, Singhal and Mamede (2013) discuss cognitive bias and debiasing in medicine. Premature closure can involve fast automatic thinking; it should not automatically be classified as motivated reasoning.
- **Legal judgement:** Sood (2013) reviews motivated cognition in legal settings. Do not infer from general debiasing experiments that every judicial safeguard or impartiality instruction is ineffective.
- **Investment decisions:** Meeuwis and colleagues (2022) link political beliefs and portfolio changes around the 2016 US election. Preserve the authors' qualification that a small share of investors making large changes drove the findings, and the limits of causal claims from observational data.
- **Close relationships:** Gagne and Lydon (2004) discuss how accuracy and positive bias vary with goals and relationship stages. This does not demonstrate that people can switch either mode on at will in unrelated settings.
- **Affective tipping points:** Redlawsk, Civettini and Emmerson (2010) studied updating after accumulated disconfirming information. An association with anxiety is not a licence to treat someone's discomfort as proof that persuasion is working.
- **Brain imaging:** Westen and colleagues (2006) studied 30 committed partisans. Poldrack (2006) explains the limits of inferring mental processes from patterns of activation. Neither a small imaging study nor an absence of a particular activation pattern demonstrates that reasoning centres have "shut down."
- **Emotion and biased evaluation:** Munro and Ditto (1997) examined affect alongside biased assimilation and polarisation. Emotional involvement and cognitive evaluation need not be competing explanations.
